Two applications that now talk to each other, the congregation's own language
on the wall, and the release where Pulpitry stops asking you questions you could
not answer.

## Twenty-two translations, in eight languages

**Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Swahili and Twi** now ship with Pulpitry, alongside
French, Spanish and Portuguese and eleven English translations — 684,000 verses,
all searched on your own machine with no network and no account.

A service preached in English can put the verse up in the language the room
reads. The two are separate settings and always were; there is simply now
something worth choosing.

Three of the English translations are new and modern: the Free Bible Version,
the Majority Standard Bible and the updated World English Bible.

Where a translation is shared under Creative Commons rather than being in the
public domain, About names who it belongs to. That is a condition of being
allowed to include it, not a courtesy.

## The spoken-language list says what can actually hear you

Thirty-seven languages, and each one now tells you which engine can transcribe
it. They genuinely differ, and the difference used to be invisible: asking a
subscription for Swahili produced confident nonsense in the wrong language with
nothing on screen admitting why.

**Yoruba and Hausa are transcribed on your own machine only** — no streaming
provider we can buy transcribes them at all. That inverts the usual advice: for
those languages the free engine is not the compromise, it is the only thing that
works, and the setting says so when you choose it.

## Pulpitry can drive Castavox's overlay

If your church runs both on two machines in the same room, they no longer both
need to listen. Pulpitry hears the sermon and stages a verse; Castavox draws it
on the streaming machine, in its own overlay design.

Only the reference and its words cross the network — never a picture of the
screen — so it works on church wifi without competing with the transcription
for bandwidth. And it means one microphone and one transcription for one sermon
instead of two.

Under Settings, **Mirror to Castavox**: switch it on, press Show a pairing code,
and type that code into Castavox. It is remembered, so next Sunday it simply
connects.

## Double-click to show

A single click still stages, so the careful path is unchanged. A double-click
stages and shows in one gesture — for the moment when you already know exactly
what belongs on the wall and clicking, then pressing Display, is two acts for
one intention.

## Transcription on your own machine is what it opens with

A fresh install used to open on Azure, which meant it opened on a demand for a
subscription key — a paywall in front of the thing that is free.

It now opens on the engine that runs on your own computer, and **chooses a
speech model for you**. If the machine cannot keep up it moves to a smaller one
by itself, rather than telling you to go and find one mid-service.

**If you already use Azure or a subscription, nothing changes.** This is for new
installs only.

## It uses the graphics card

On Apple silicon, and on Windows PCs with almost any graphics — Intel, AMD or
NVIDIA. Where there is nothing suitable it runs on the processor as before.

There is a **Time it** button beside the model: it decodes for a few seconds and
tells you how fast this machine actually is, which is worth knowing on a Tuesday
rather than during a sermon.

## A second congregation display

The projector can be joined by a second display on another screen entirely — a
foyer, an overflow room, a wall behind the choir. It shows the real thing rather
than an approximation of it, and you choose which monitor each one lands on.

## Slides from a sermon outline

Paste the preacher's notes and a draft set of slides comes back: short titles, a
few words to a line, legible from the back of a hall.

Nothing is saved until you have read it, and each slide can be dropped on its
own — the usual outcome is six good ones and one that missed the point. What you
keep is saved as a group, the way a song is, rather than scattered through a
library sorted by title.

Scripture stays a reference. Pulpitry holds twenty-two translations and a model
quoting from memory gets a verse subtly wrong in front of the people most likely
to notice, so the words come from your own library.

## Send the screen out over NDI

For a church whose video team runs a switcher, a second machine, or software
that is not Castavox: Pulpitry publishes what is on the projector as an NDI
source on the network. No capture card, no HDMI run across the building.

**This is not the same as Mirror, and the two are easy to confuse.** Mirror
sends a *reference* — a few dozen bytes — and works on wifi. This sends the
actual picture, about 100 Mbit/s for 1080p, and wants a wired network. Use
Mirror to put verses on a Castavox; use this when the pixels themselves matter.

Off unless you switch it on. The NDI runtime is free but is Vizrt's to give
rather than ours to include, so it is installed separately — Settings says so
if it is missing.

## A menu, and an About that says what is inside

Pulpitry had whatever menu bar Tauri supplies by default, which on macOS is
worse than none: it is the first place anybody looks. It now has its own —
screens, the microphone and what is on the wall, with the shortcuts an operator
needs in a hurry.

About lists what is inside: whose speech model, whose translations, whose hymns.

## Smaller things

- Verses appear sooner on a fast machine. The pace follows what your hardware
  can do rather than a fixed two seconds.
- The settings dialog no longer demands a speech model from a subscription,
  which never used one.
- An unlimited plan says "Unlimited" rather than "1000000 hr left this period",
  and drops the progress bar that was pinned at zero for ever.
- The renewal date shows the year. "Renews 14 Aug" reads as this week, which is
  eleven months out on a yearly plan.
- A new licence key can be typed straight over the old one, without removing it
  first and hoping the new one works.

## Installing

Not code signed. macOS will refuse to open it until you right-click and choose
Open; Windows SmartScreen will warn.

The engine that runs on your own machine needs no Node runtime. Azure and
Castavox subscriptions still need Node 20 or later, which is not bundled.
Scripture, songs, slides and the projector work without it.
